When museums discuss the return of objects, the dispute is often reduced to whether one item will be possessed or lost. In reality, a decision to return an object also affects how research materials, conservation skills and public narratives will continue to circulate. The community of origin may want the object to come home while worrying that local preservation facilities are inadequate; the collecting institution may stress that open databases and long-term research have made it part of a transnational knowledge resource. If both sides argue only about ownership, they may overlook a crucial point: digital copies can widen access, but they cannot replace recognition of ritual uses, historical wounds or the community's authority to decide. A more careful arrangement could negotiate the physical transfer, joint custody, staff training and data licences separately, while allowing the agreement to be reviewed after several years in light of preservation results. Such an arrangement does not turn a moral issue into a technical one. It acknowledges that repairing a relationship requires continuing institutional work and cannot be completed by a single transfer document.
